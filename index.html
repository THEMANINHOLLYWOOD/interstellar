INTERSTELLAR

one hour here · seven years at home

The math

This world orbits so close to a spinning black hole that one hour here is seven years at home. That is about 61,000 to 1.

7 years ÷ 1 hour ≈ 61,360
1 Earth day ÷ 61,360 ≈ 1.41 s

So every tick you hear is one day passing on Earth, and the cockpit’s second clock counts them. Fans have timed the ticking in the film’s score at about one tick every 1.25 seconds; at seven years an hour that is about 21 hours at home per tick, roughly a day. The filmmakers have not confirmed it.

Kip Thorne’s figures behind the ratio: the black hole has about 100 million times the Sun’s mass and spins within about one part in a hundred trillion of the fastest a black hole can spin. The planet orbits just outside the horizon, once every 1.7 hours as seen from far away.

Gravity is 130% of Earth’s, the figure given in the film. Everything weighs 1.3 times as much, so in air of the same density a wing has to fly √1.3 ≈ 1.14 times faster to carry it, and a fall from the same height lands 14% faster, with 30% more energy.

g = 1.3 × 9.81 ≈ 12.75 m/s²
stall speed = √(2 × weight ÷ (air density × wing area × lift))
≈ 58 m/s here; the same lander stalls at 51 m/s on Earth

The wave is about 1.2 km (4,000 ft) tall, Thorne’s figure. He offers two explanations and does not choose between them. Either the planet is almost but not quite tidally locked, and rocks back and forth under the black hole’s pull about once an hour, so the ocean sloshes with it (rather like a tidal bore on an enormous scale); or the hourly flexing of the crust sets off quakes that raise giant tsunamis. Where the lander comes down the water is only knee-deep.

How fast does it come? Treated as a single, solitary wave, it runs at about √(g × (depth + height)).

c ≈ √(12.75 × 1,200.5) ≈ 124 m/s ≈ 445 km/h
one an hour: 124 m/s × 3,600 s ≈ 445 km between crests

From 5 km away the crest arrives in about 40 seconds. Sitting in front of it, the water under you rises faster every second: where the wave is 300 m deep, the surface is climbing at about 60 m/s. That is why the lander has to leave the face early.

What is assumed. The film and the book give the time dilation, the gravity, the wave’s height and the shallow water. The rest is this page’s model, and some of it is a stretch:

That speed is a rough guide only. A solitary wave taller than about 0.8 of the water’s depth breaks, so a 1.2 km wave cannot be carried by knee-deep water: it would need water about 1.5 km deep. Thorne suggests the waves come in from deeper ocean; here, as in the film, the shallow sea simply has them. A bore (a travelling step in the water, like a tide running up a river) would, by the usual formula and with these heights, run at more than 4 km/s, which is why the page does not use it. The wave’s shape is ours: its front grows e-fold every 600 m, which makes the face about 63° steep at the crest.

The planet is a stretch too: a black hole spinning that close to its limit is allowed but not expected, and starlight and the glow of the early universe falling into gravity this deep would arrive so strongly blue-shifted that the surface would be far too hot to live on. The page leaves that out, as the film does.

The air is assumed to be as dense as Earth’s at the surface (1.2 kg/m³) and at the same temperature, thinning with a scale height of 8.5 km ÷ 1.3 ≈ 6.5 km. The lander is invented: 9 tonnes, 46 m² of wing, air brakes, floats, and engines that give 12% of its weight on the way down and 2.6 times its weight (3.4 Earth g) on the way up. Close to the sea its wing rides a cushion of air (ground effect), which cuts the drag. Water flooding the engine intakes at splash-down, and draining out as the wave tips the nose up, is our invention too.

You arrive late in the hour between waves. Arriving early would mean waiting most of an hour for the next one, and years at home.

The lander Heron, its cockpit, the companion WREN and the hoop it rolls out in are original designs; the boat and its crew are from Adrift. Every sound is made here from oscillators and noise: a clock and an organ, engines, rain, thunder, switches, alarms. Nothing is taken from the film.
